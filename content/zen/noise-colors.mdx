### What Are Noise Colors?

A noise color describes where a steady sound puts its energy. **[White noise](/blog/the-power-of-white-noise)** spreads it evenly across every frequency, so it sounds bright and hissy. Pink noise gives each octave the same energy, which sounds softer and more balanced. Brown noise leans heavily on the low end and sounds like a deep rumble or distant surf. Grey and green noise sit in between, shaped for how the ear hears rather than for a formula.

This matters for **[tinnitus](/blog/what-is-tinnitus)** because a steady sound blends best with a ringing or hissing that sits in the same range. A high-pitched whistle is easiest to soften with a noise that reaches the high frequencies, while a deep rumble suits someone who mainly wants a calm, warm background. If you are not sure which suits you, our guide to **[brown noise vs white noise for tinnitus](/blog/brown-noise-vs-white-noise-for-tinnitus)** walks through the difference.

Every track in _Colors of Calm_ is generated rather than recorded, so there are no birds, voices or sudden sounds, only a smooth bed of noise that moves very slowly in level to keep it from feeling static. Each track runs ten minutes.

### Which Color Is Right for Your Tinnitus?

Because these tracks are generated, we measured exactly where each one puts its sound. The table shows the share of each track's energy in each frequency band:

| Track            | Below 200 Hz | 200 Hz to 1 kHz | 1 to 4 kHz | 4 to 8 kHz | Above 8 kHz |
| ---------------- | ------------ | --------------- | ---------- | ---------- | ----------- |
| **Silver Veil**  | 2%           | 7%              | 25%        | 32%        | 35%         |
| **Rose Haze**    | 42%          | 20%             | 17%        | 9%         | 13%         |
| **Grey Balance** | 16%          | 7%              | 8%         | 13%        | 56%         |
| **Green Meadow** | 17%          | 56%             | 26%        | 1%         | 0%          |
| **Deep Brown**   | 87%          | 10%             | 2%         | 0%         | 0%          |
| **Velvet Night** | 93%          | 8%              | 0%         | 0%         | 0%          |

- **High-pitched ringing or whistling:** start with _Silver Veil_ or _Grey Balance_. Two thirds of their sound sits above 4 kHz, the range where many people hear their tinnitus.
- **Not sure, or a mix of sounds:** try _Rose Haze_. Pink noise covers the whole range without the hiss of white noise.
- **Comfort, calm and sleep:** choose _Green Meadow_, _Deep Brown_ or _Velvet Night_. They do little to cover a high tone, but many people find them easier to listen to for a long time.
- **Sound sensitivity:** if bright sounds bother you, as they often do with **[hyperacusis](/blog/hyperacusis-and-tinnitus)**, begin with the darker tracks at a low volume.

### How to Enjoy _Colors of Calm_ for Maximum Relaxation

#### <Highlighter>Find a Tranquil Space:</Highlighter>

Listen somewhere comfortable where you will not be interrupted, such as your bedroom before sleep or a quiet room during a break. A calm setting lets the steady sound settle in.

#### <Highlighter>Select Your Device and Platform:</Highlighter>

You can stream _Colors of Calm_ on Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube Music, Amazon Music, and Deezer. Use whatever suits you best, whether that is a phone, a tablet, a computer or a smart speaker.

#### <Highlighter>Start With Two Colors:</Highlighter>

Play one bright track and one dark track, for example _Silver Veil_ and _Deep Brown_, for a minute each. Notice which one blends better with your tinnitus and which one feels more comfortable, then explore the colors in between.

<AdComponent />

#### <Highlighter>Set the Volume Just Below Your Tinnitus:</Highlighter>

Turn the noise up until your tinnitus starts to blend into it, then stop. You should still be able to hear your tinnitus a little. The aim is to soften it and take away its edge, not to drown it out, and a lower volume is also kinder to your ears over a long session.

#### <Highlighter>Use Good [Headphones](/blog/noise-canceling-headphones-for-tinnitus) or Speakers:</Highlighter>

Every track is in stereo, with a slightly different sound in each ear, which makes it feel like a room rather than a point between your ears. Comfortable headphones or a pair of speakers bring that out best. For sleep, a speaker on the bedside table is often more comfortable than headphones.

#### <Highlighter>Let It Loop:</Highlighter>

Put the album or a single track on repeat. The volume drifts very slowly, so the sound never feels frozen, and there are no sudden changes to pull your attention back to it.

#### <Highlighter>Practice Regularly for Cumulative Benefits:</Highlighter>

Consistency often helps more than any single session. Try one color in the same moment each day, such as when you start work or when the house goes quiet at night, and adjust the choice and the volume as you learn what works for you.

### Colors of Calm for Tinnitus Relief: Choosing by Sound, Not by Name

Most noise tracks are sold by their color alone, but two tracks with the same name can sound very different. That is why we measured ours and published the numbers. If your tinnitus sits high, a track with almost nothing above 4 kHz will feel pleasant but will not do much to soften the ringing, and the table above tells you that before you press play.

These sounds are a tool for **[managing tinnitus](/blog/managing-tinnitus)** and for relaxing, not a treatment. Results vary from person to person, and if your tinnitus is new, changes suddenly, or is in one ear only, it is worth seeing a doctor or an ear specialist.
