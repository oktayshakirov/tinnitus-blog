## <Highlighter>What is the 2026 tinnitus guideline?</Highlighter>

The 2026 tinnitus guideline is the **Clinical Practice Guideline: Tinnitus Update**, published on October 12, 2026 by the American Academy of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery Foundation. It tells clinicians to give everyone with tinnitus a hearing evaluation, ask about mood and self-harm, and offer hearing aids and CBT, and it advises against routine drugs, supplements, rTMS and unnecessary scans.

If you have lived with ringing for a while, you may have heard very different advice from different people. One doctor suggests a supplement, another orders a scan, a third tells you to learn to live with it. A guideline like this one exists to close that gap, so the care you get depends less on which clinic you happen to walk into.

This article walks through who the guideline applies to, all 16 recommendations in one table, what is genuinely new compared with the 2014 version, and the questions worth taking to your next appointment. If your tinnitus only started recently, **[new tinnitus: what to do in the first week](/blog/new-tinnitus-what-to-do-first-week)** is the better place to begin.

## <Highlighter>Who the guideline is for</Highlighter>

The guideline is written for clinicians, which includes family doctors, ENT specialists, audiologists and psychologists. Its patient group is narrower than you might expect: adults aged 18 and over whose tinnitus is both **persistent**, meaning it has lasted six months or longer, and **bothersome**, meaning it affects their quality of life.

Some groups are deliberately left out. The guideline does not cover the full management of **pulsatile tinnitus** (a rhythmic sound) or **objective tinnitus** (a sound someone else can hear too). It also does not apply to the brief ringing in one ear that fades within five minutes, which the authors note does not usually need treatment. People whose tinnitus has a clear cause other than hearing loss, such as **[Ménière's disease](/blog/menieres-disease-and-tinnitus)**, are outside its main scope too, because they are excluded from nearly all the trials it is built on.

The update was written by a panel that included ENT surgeons, audiologists, a neurologist, a psychiatrist, a radiologist and a consumer advocate from the American Tinnitus Association. According to the AAO-HNS summary, its recommendations draw on 28 earlier guidelines, 223 systematic reviews and 362 randomized controlled trials.

##### Why it matters

The guideline estimates that 10% to 15% of adults have tinnitus, and more than 50 million people in the United States have reported it. Yet the AAO-HNS summary notes that only about half of adults with tinnitus discuss it with a clinician, and about 20% seek treatment. Clear, shared standards are one way to make that visit worth having.

## <Highlighter>The 16 recommendations at a glance</Highlighter>

Each recommendation is called a **key action statement** and carries a strength. A strong recommendation means the benefits clearly outweigh the harms and the evidence is good. A plain recommendation is still a firm "should". An option means clinicians may do it, but the evidence is less certain or the balance of benefit and harm is closer.

## <Highlighter>What changed since the 2014 guideline</Highlighter>

Put the two versions side by side and the direction is clear: more attention on the person, less on pills and scans.

##### A hearing evaluation for everyone

In 2014, a comprehensive hearing test was a firm recommendation only for tinnitus in one ear, tinnitus lasting six months or more, or tinnitus with hearing problems. For everyone else it was an option. The 2026 version makes it a recommendation for all patients with tinnitus. The guideline's lead author, Dr. Meredith Adams of the University of Michigan, has said that every patient should get one. **[How audiologists diagnose and treat tinnitus](/blog/how-audiologists-diagnose-and-treat-tinnitus)** explains what that appointment involves.

##### Mood and safety are now part of the exam

The biggest new addition is a strong recommendation that clinicians ask about the risk of self-harm. The guideline's patient materials note that up to one in three people with tinnitus may meet the criteria for major depression. If you have noticed how much ringing can wear down sleep and mood, **[tinnitus impact on mental health](/blog/tinnitus-impact-on-mental-health)** covers that link in more depth.

##### Wider access to hearing devices

The 2014 guideline tied the hearing aid recommendation to documented hearing loss. The update widens it to anyone with hearing difficulties and persistent, bothersome tinnitus. It also adds a new recommendation to refer people for a **cochlear implant** evaluation when their hearing loss is severe enough to meet the criteria.

##### Newer treatments get a mention

**Bimodal neuromodulation**, which did not feature in 2014, is now an option, as is asking about **somatosensory** features, where the sound changes when you move your head, neck or jaw.

##### Follow-up becomes the standard

The update adds a recommendation that clinicians measure and record whether a treatment is helping. In other words, you should not be sent away with a plan and never asked how it went.

## <Highlighter>What the guideline recommends</Highlighter>

Here is what each of the main recommendations means for you as a patient.

##### Education comes first

Clinicians should explain what tinnitus is, how it tends to behave over time and what you can do about it. That sounds basic, but understanding that the sound is common, rarely dangerous and often easier to live with over time is a treatment in its own right.

##### Hearing aids when hearing is a struggle

Many people with tinnitus also have some hearing loss, and amplifying the sounds around you can make the ringing less noticeable. **[Hearing aids and tinnitus](/blog/hearing-aids-and-tinnitus)** covers who benefits and what to ask for.

##### Cognitive behavioral therapy

CBT does not aim to switch the sound off. It works on how you react to it: the worry, the attention it grabs and the effect on sleep and mood. It is one of only a handful of treatments the guideline actively recommends, and **[cognitive behavioral therapy and tinnitus](/blog/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-and-tinnitus)** explains how it works in practice.

##### Sound enrichment

The 2014 guideline called it sound therapy. The update calls it sound enrichment and keeps it as an option. The patient guide's everyday version is simple: add gentle background sound to quiet rooms, such as a fan or soft music. A low, steady sound like **[white noise](/zen/white-noise)** is one easy way to try it.

##### Bimodal neuromodulation

This approach pairs sound with mild stimulation of another nerve, usually through the tongue. As an option, it is something a clinician may offer, not something you should expect to be prescribed first. **[Bimodal neuromodulation for tinnitus](/blog/bimodal-neuromodulation-for-tinnitus)** covers the devices and what the evidence shows.

##### Checking for neck and jaw involvement

Clinicians may ask whether clenching your jaw, turning your head or pressing on your neck changes the sound. If it does, **[somatic tinnitus](/blog/somatic-tinnitus)** may be part of the picture, which opens up different management options.

## <Highlighter>What the guideline advises against</Highlighter>

These are the parts of the update most likely to surprise people, because they cover treatments that are widely sold or prescribed.

##### Routine medication for tinnitus itself

Clinicians should not routinely recommend antidepressants, anticonvulsants, anti-anxiety medicines or drugs injected through the eardrum when the main goal is to treat tinnitus. The key words are "for a primary indication". If you also have depression or anxiety, treating that condition is a separate decision, and **[do antidepressants make tinnitus worse](/blog/do-antidepressants-make-tinnitus-worse)** looks at that question. Do not stop any prescribed medicine without speaking to your prescriber.

##### Dietary supplements

The guideline advises against recommending supplements for tinnitus. The 2014 version named ginkgo, melatonin and zinc, and the 2026 patient guide again uses ginkgo as its example, which matches what we found in **[ginkgo biloba and tinnitus](/blog/ginkgo-biloba-and-tinnitus)**. The patient guide also warns that some treatments do not help and can be costly.

##### Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation

rTMS uses magnetic pulses applied to the scalp to stimulate the brain. The 2014 guideline advised against it for routine treatment. The 2026 wording drops the word "routine" and simply says clinicians should not recommend it.

##### Scans without warning signs

The guideline keeps its strong recommendation against brain imaging done only to investigate tinnitus. Most people need a hearing test, not an MRI or CT scan.

## <Highlighter>Warning signs that still need prompt care</Highlighter>

Advising against routine scans does not mean ignoring the rare cases where tinnitus points to something serious. The guideline notes that tinnitus can occasionally be a symptom of conditions such as a **vestibular schwannoma**, a slow-growing tumour on the hearing nerve, or a vascular tumour. The patient guide lists these signs as reasons for further testing or prompt care:

- Tinnitus in one ear only
- Tinnitus that pulses, often in time with your heartbeat, as described in **[pulsatile tinnitus](/blog/pulsatile-tinnitus-why-you-hear-your-heartbeat)**
- Hearing loss in one ear, or hearing loss that comes on suddenly, which needs same-day attention as **[sudden hearing loss and tinnitus](/blog/sudden-hearing-loss-and-tinnitus)** explains
- New dizziness, weakness, numbness or balance problems
- Thoughts of self-harm

If you are having thoughts of harming yourself, please reach out today. In the US you can call or text 988, in the UK and Ireland you can call Samaritans on 116 123, and anywhere you can contact your local emergency services.

## <Highlighter>How to use the guideline at your next appointment</Highlighter>

You do not need to quote key action statements at your doctor. But knowing what good care looks like makes it easier to ask for it. These questions map directly onto the update:

- Can I have a comprehensive hearing test, not just a quick screening?
- Would a hearing aid help me, and do I qualify for a cochlear implant evaluation?
- Can you refer me for cognitive behavioral therapy for tinnitus?
- Does my tinnitus change with neck or jaw movement, and does that matter?
- Is there a specific reason I need a scan, or would a hearing test answer the question?
- How will we measure whether this treatment is working, and when do we review it?

The last question matters more than it looks. The guideline asks clinicians to track outcomes, and you can help by keeping your own record. A short daily note of how loud and how bothersome the sound feels, alongside sleep and stress, gives you something concrete to bring to a follow-up visit. The **[Tinnitus Help app](/app)** has a tracker built for exactly this.

## <Highlighter>Conclusion: a guideline built around the person, not the pill</Highlighter>

The 2026 update does not announce a cure, and it is honest that no single treatment works for everyone. What it does is set a clearer standard: a proper hearing evaluation for everyone, real attention to mood and safety, hearing devices and CBT where they help, and fewer drugs, supplements and scans that rarely change the outcome.

If your own care so far has not looked like this, you are entitled to ask why. Take the questions above to your next appointment, keep track of how you are doing, and build steady habits from **[managing tinnitus](/blog/managing-tinnitus)** alongside whatever your clinician recommends. Tinnitus is common and often hard, but it is also, in the patient guide's words, something whose symptoms can often be managed successfully.
