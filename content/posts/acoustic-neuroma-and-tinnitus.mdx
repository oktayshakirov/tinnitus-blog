## <Highlighter>Can an acoustic neuroma cause tinnitus?</Highlighter>
Yes. An acoustic neuroma is a benign tumour on the nerve that carries hearing and balance signals from the inner ear to the brain, and tinnitus in the affected ear is one of its typical early signs, usually alongside hearing loss on that side. It is also rare, and most one-sided tinnitus has a far more ordinary cause.

If you have landed here after hearing a new sound in one ear, that second sentence matters as much as the first. The tumour is real and worth knowing about, but it sits at the rare end of a long list of explanations. The useful question is not "could this be a tumour?" but "what would make a doctor check for one?", and that question has a clear answer.

This guide covers what an acoustic neuroma is, why it produces tinnitus, which symptom combinations change the picture, how the condition is diagnosed and treated, and what tends to happen to the ringing afterwards. If you are new to the subject, **[what tinnitus is](/blog/what-is-tinnitus)** is a good place to start first.

## <Highlighter>What is an acoustic neuroma?</Highlighter>
An **acoustic neuroma**, also called a **vestibular schwannoma**, is a non-cancerous tumour that grows from the nerve supplying the inner ear. According to the NIDCD, it develops from an overproduction of **Schwann cells**, the cells that wrap around nerve fibres to support and insulate them. It usually grows slowly over many years and, unlike a cancer, does not spread to other parts of the body.

Both names describe the same thing. Doctors tend to say "vestibular schwannoma", after the vestibular (balance) nerve and the Schwann cells it grows from, while "acoustic neuroma" is the older name most people search for.

Some numbers help put it in proportion. The NIDCD estimates that about **one person in 100,000** develops one each year, and that one-sided acoustic neuromas account for roughly 8 percent of all tumours inside the skull. Symptoms can appear at any age but usually show up between 30 and 60.

##### Is it hereditary?

Most one-sided acoustic neuromas are not inherited and appear for no known reason. The exception is a genetic condition called **neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2)**, which usually causes tumours on the nerves of both ears. The NIDCD notes that about half of people with NF2 inherited it from a parent, that each child of an affected parent has a 50 percent chance of inheriting it, and that symptoms usually begin in the teens or early adulthood. For the wider question of family links, see **[whether tinnitus is hereditary](/blog/is-tinnitus-hereditary)**.

## <Highlighter>Why an acoustic neuroma causes tinnitus</Highlighter>
The tumour grows directly on the nerve your inner ear uses to send sound to the brain. As it grows, it affects the hearing and balance nerves, and the NIDCD lists one-sided or uneven hearing loss, tinnitus and dizziness or loss of balance as the usual results. The NIDCD's main tinnitus page names acoustic neuromas, along with other head, neck and brain tumours, among the conditions that can cause tinnitus.

Hearing loss is the thread connecting these symptoms. Most tinnitus, whatever its cause, travels with some degree of hearing loss, and our guide to **[tinnitus and hearing loss](/blog/tinnitus-and-hearing-loss)** explains why the two so often appear together. With an acoustic neuroma, the difference is that both tend to affect just one side.

##### Early signs

The NHS describes symptoms that tend to develop gradually and often include:

- Hearing loss that usually affects only one ear
- Tinnitus, heard in that ear
- Vertigo, the sensation that you or the room is moving or spinning

The NIDCD adds an honest caveat: early detection can be difficult, because symptoms may be subtle and may not appear at all while the tumour is small.

##### Signs of a larger tumour

As the tumour grows, it can press on nearby nerves and brain structures. The NHS lists temporary blurred or double vision, numbness, pain or weakness on one side of the face, problems with coordination on one side of the body, and changes to the voice or difficulty swallowing. The NHS also stresses that it is rare for a tumour to reach this stage, because many grow very slowly or not at all, and those that grow faster can usually be treated before they become large.

## <Highlighter>How rare is it, really?</Highlighter>
Ringing in one ear is common. Acoustic neuromas are not. The NIDCD makes the key point well: hearing loss, dizziness and tinnitus are symptoms of many middle and inner ear problems, and it is specifically the one-sided or uneven pattern that makes these symptoms worth investigating.

Most of the time, that pattern has an everyday explanation. **[Earwax](/blog/earwax-and-tinnitus)** pressing on one eardrum, a lingering infection, or more noise exposure on one side over the years are all far more likely. Our guide to **[tinnitus in one ear](/blog/tinnitus-in-one-ear)** walks through those common causes in detail.

The NHS also points out that acoustic neuromas can be hard to tell apart from other conditions by symptoms alone, and names **[Meniere's disease](/blog/menieres-disease-and-tinnitus)** as one that looks similar, since it also combines one-sided tinnitus, hearing changes and vertigo. That overlap is exactly why testing, not guessing, settles the question.

| Pattern | What it usually points to |
| --- | --- |
| Tinnitus in both ears, hearing similar on both sides | Usually age or noise-related tinnitus; a one-sided tumour is unlikely |
| Tinnitus in one ear, normal hearing test | Usually an everyday cause such as wax, infection or jaw and neck tension, or no clear cause |
| Tinnitus in one ear with hearing loss on that side | Needs proper assessment; this is the pattern where a scan may be considered |
| Sudden hearing loss, facial weakness or vertigo | Needs urgent same-day care, whatever the cause |

## <Highlighter>How doctors rule it in or out</Highlighter>
The path to a diagnosis is more straightforward than most people fear, and for nearly everyone it ends without finding a tumour.

It starts with a history and an ear examination, then a **hearing test** that measures each ear separately. The 2026 US tinnitus guideline update now recommends a comprehensive hearing evaluation for every patient with tinnitus, so this test is standard rather than a special step. Our guide to **[how audiologists diagnose and treat tinnitus](/blog/how-audiologists-diagnose-and-treat-tinnitus)** explains what the appointment involves.

The hearing test is the filter. If your two ears hear about equally well, the chance of an acoustic neuroma is low. If one ear is clearly worse, the NIDCD describes further hearing and balance tests and an **MRI scan**, which it calls critical for finding these tumours early and for measuring their size and position. The NHS adds that a CT scan is sometimes used instead.

Scans are deliberately not offered to everyone with tinnitus. The guideline limits imaging to people with specific concerning features rather than ordering it routinely, and our summary of the **[2026 tinnitus guideline](/blog/tinnitus-guideline-2026)** covers that recommendation and the warning signs behind it.

##### Seek urgent care the same day if you have

The NHS advises going to A&E or calling emergency services if tinnitus comes with:

- Sudden hearing loss, which is time-critical for reasons explained in **[sudden hearing loss and tinnitus](/blog/sudden-hearing-loss-and-tinnitus)**
- Weakness in the muscles of your face
- A spinning sensation (vertigo)
- A recent head injury

The NHS also advises an urgent GP appointment if your tinnitus beats in time with your pulse.

##### Book a routine appointment if

- New tinnitus has stayed in one ear for more than a few weeks
- Your hearing on one side feels different from the other, even slightly
- Your tinnitus is getting worse or affecting your sleep or mood

## <Highlighter>Treatment options for an acoustic neuroma</Highlighter>
Finding an acoustic neuroma does not automatically mean surgery. The NIDCD and the NHS both describe three approaches, chosen according to the tumour's size and position, how fast it is growing, your hearing, and your general health.

| Approach | What it involves | When it is usually chosen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Monitoring** | Regular MRI scans to check for growth | Small tumours that are not growing |
| **Stereotactic radiosurgery** | A precise beam of radiation aimed at the tumour to stop it growing | Small tumours, pieces left after surgery, or when surgery carries more risk |
| **Surgery** | Removing all or part of the tumour under general anaesthetic | Larger tumours, or tumours that are getting bigger |

Monitoring surprises people the most, but it is common. The NHS explains that small tumours often only need regular MRI scans, with treatment recommended if the scans show growth. The NIDCD notes that radiosurgery is sometimes the preferred option for older people, people in poor health, people with tumours on both sides, and people whose tumour affects their only hearing ear.

Every option carries some risk. The NHS lists facial numbness or paralysis of part of the face as possible effects of both surgery and radiosurgery. The NHS also estimates that a tumour comes back in around 1 in every 20 people who have it surgically removed, which is why follow-up MRI scans usually continue after treatment. These decisions belong with a specialist team who can weigh the details of your scan.

## <Highlighter>What happens to the tinnitus after treatment?</Highlighter>
This is the part most articles skip, and it is the part people with a diagnosis most want to know. The honest answer is that treating the tumour does not reliably remove the tinnitus.

The NHS states plainly that symptoms such as hearing loss and tinnitus can persist even after treatment, and can affect your ability to work, communicate and drive. The NIDCD adds that removing tumours from the hearing, balance or facial nerves can sometimes make symptoms worse, because those nerves may be injured during the operation. On the other side, the NIDCD notes that removing a small tumour may preserve hearing and improve the accompanying symptoms.

So it is worth going into treatment expecting that you may need to manage tinnitus afterwards, rather than hoping it will simply stop. The good news is that the tools for persistent tinnitus work here too:

- **Hearing support.** If you have lost hearing on one side, amplification can reduce how prominent the tinnitus feels. Ask your team whether **[hearing aids](/blog/hearing-aids-and-tinnitus)** or other devices suit your situation, since options differ when one ear has little usable hearing.
- **Sound enrichment.** Gentle background sound keeps tinnitus from standing out against silence, especially at night. Our guide to **[white noise](/blog/the-power-of-white-noise)** covers how to use it well.
- **Psychological support.** A diagnosis and treatment of a tumour, even a benign one, is stressful. **[Cognitive behavioral therapy](/blog/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-and-tinnitus)** is one of the treatments the 2026 guideline update recommends for tinnitus that is persistent and bothersome, because it reduces how much the sound disrupts your life.

## <Highlighter>Conclusion: a rare cause, worth one simple check</Highlighter>
An acoustic neuroma is one of the few causes of tinnitus that clinicians actively screen for, and that is reassuring rather than alarming. The screening is a hearing test, the tumour is rare, it is benign, and most are found while they are small and slow-growing enough to simply watch.

If your tinnitus is in one ear, the sensible step is a hearing test in the coming weeks. If it arrives with sudden hearing loss, facial weakness or vertigo, get care the same day. Otherwise, try not to fill the waiting time with worst-case reading, because the most likely outcome by far is an ordinary explanation.

And if you do receive a diagnosis, you are not facing the tinnitus side of it alone. The same strategies that help millions of people live well with tinnitus apply after treatment, and if worry about your health is taking over, **[staying mentally strong](/blog/tinnitus-and-anxiety-staying-mentally-strong)** is a good next read.
