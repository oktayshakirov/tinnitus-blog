## <Highlighter>Can Ozempic cause tinnitus?</Highlighter>

There is no proof that it does. Tinnitus is not listed as a side effect on the Ozempic label, and it is not among the side effects reported in the trials summarised there. People have reported ringing after starting GLP-1 drugs, and researchers have found a weak reporting signal, but mostly for an older drug in the class rather than semaglutide itself.

That is not the same as "it is all in your head." If your ears started ringing a few weeks after your first injection, the timing is real and worth taking seriously. It may be the drug, it may be something the drug changed, such as rapid weight loss or days of nausea, or it may be a coincidence that happened to land at the same time.

This guide walks through what the drug label says, what the adverse event data and the newer hearing studies found, one ear problem that is genuinely linked to fast weight loss, and a practical plan if your **tinnitus** appeared or got louder on treatment. If the condition itself is new to you, start with **[what tinnitus is](/blog/what-is-tinnitus)**.

## <Highlighter>What Ozempic is and why the question keeps coming up</Highlighter>

Ozempic is the brand name for **semaglutide**, a **GLP-1 receptor agonist** given as a weekly injection for type 2 diabetes. The same drug is sold as Wegovy for weight loss. Tirzepatide (Mounjaro and Zepbound) works on the same pathway plus a second hormone, and liraglutide (Victoza and Saxenda) is an older daily member of the family.

Because so many people now take these medicines, even a rare symptom adds up to a lot of people searching for answers. And tinnitus is common in the general population anyway, so some people will notice it for the first time while on any drug, purely by chance. Sorting signal from coincidence is exactly the hard part.

It also helps to know how medicines usually get linked to ear symptoms. Some drugs are well established as harmful to the inner ear, which our post on **[ototoxic medications and tinnitus](/blog/ototoxic-medications-and-tinnitus)** covers. GLP-1 drugs are not on that list.

## <Highlighter>What the label and the trials say</Highlighter>

The US prescribing information for Ozempic lists nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain and constipation as the most common side effects, each reported by at least 5% of people in the trials. Tinnitus, hearing loss and vertigo do not appear anywhere in it. The same is true of the current labels for Wegovy, Mounjaro, Zepbound, Victoza and Saxenda.

Two warnings on the label are still relevant to your ears in an indirect way:

- **Dehydration.** The label warns that vomiting and diarrhea can lead to volume depletion and asks doctors to monitor kidney function, especially when the dose is started or increased.
- **Low blood sugar.** Taken together with insulin or a sulfonylurea, semaglutide can raise the risk of hypoglycemia, which may mean those other doses need lowering.

Neither is a proven tinnitus cause, but both are the kind of whole-body change that often coincides with a bad week for people who already live with ringing. If you are vomiting often, keeping fluids up matters for many reasons, and our guide to **[hydration and tinnitus](/blog/hydration-and-tinnitus-the-importance-of-water-intake)** covers the everyday side of that.

## <Highlighter>What the FDA reports show</Highlighter>

Most of the evidence on GLP-1 drugs and the ear comes from the **FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS)**, where patients and clinicians can report a symptom they suspect is a side effect. It is a useful early warning system, but it has two big limits: anyone can report, and nobody counts the millions of people who took the drug and had no problem. A report shows that two things happened to the same person, not that one caused the other.

With that in mind, here is what two 2025 analyses found:

| Study | What it looked at | Tinnitus finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Khan et al., *The Laryngoscope* | Ear, nose and throat reports for five GLP-1 drugs through the end of 2023 | Tinnitus showed a significant reporting signal for **liraglutide**. Semaglutide's notable signals were for smell, taste, dry mouth and Bell's palsy. |
| Pak et al., *Otology & Neurotology* | All 97,237 adverse event reports for GLP-1 drugs | 958 reports (0.99%) involved the ear, and only 93 of those mentioned tinnitus. Reduced hearing and vertigo were far more common. |

The American Academy of Otolaryngology's own bulletin reviewed this work in 2025 and described the strength of the ear associations as weak, well below the link between these drugs and acid reflux. It also pointed out that the links were not class-wide, and that a network analysis of clinical trials found higher hearing loss rates only with two GLP-1 drugs that are not sold in the US. Its conclusion was that proper studies with standard hearing tests are still needed.

## <Highlighter>What the newer hearing studies found</Highlighter>

A 2026 study in the *International Journal of Medical Sciences* went a step further than report counting. Using US health records, it matched 342,046 people starting a GLP-1 drug with the same number starting a different class of diabetes medicine and followed them for up to nine years.

The GLP-1 group was diagnosed with **hearing impairment** slightly more often. After ten years, about 10% of the GLP-1 group had a hearing impairment diagnosis compared with about 8% of the comparison group, a hazard ratio of 1.17. In plain terms, that is a small relative increase, not a dramatic one.

Three caveats matter here. The study did not measure tinnitus as its own outcome. It had no hearing test results, only diagnosis codes. And the authors themselves say the link may be observational rather than causal, since people on GLP-1 drugs may differ in ways the records could not capture. Hearing loss and tinnitus are closely connected, as our post on **[tinnitus and hearing loss](/blog/tinnitus-and-hearing-loss)** explains, so this is worth watching, but it does not settle the question.

## <Highlighter>The ear problem that is linked to fast weight loss</Highlighter>

There is one ear condition where the connection is better understood: the **patulous Eustachian tube**. The Eustachian tube runs from your middle ear to the back of your nose and normally stays closed, opening briefly when you swallow or yawn. It is cushioned by soft tissue, including fat. When that tissue thins, the tube can stay open.

Doctors have known for years that this can follow major weight loss, especially after bariatric surgery. In 2025 two reports described it after GLP-1 treatment. In the Pak study, camera examination of two patients showed clear loss of tissue around the tube. A separate German case series described seven adults who developed it 4 to 10 months into semaglutide or tirzepatide treatment, after losing between 8% and 19% of their body weight.

The symptoms are distinctive, and they are not quite the same as classic ringing:

- Hearing your own voice unusually loud, as if you are talking into a barrel (called **autophony**)
- Hearing your own breathing in your ear
- A full or blocked feeling that comes and goes

In the case series, every patient had the loud own-voice symptom. One improved with simple measures and six needed a small procedure to bulk up the tube, after which their symptoms resolved. If what you notice sounds more like this than like a steady ring or hiss, mention the weight loss and these exact symptoms to your doctor or an ENT. Ear pressure from other causes, such as flying, works differently, as **[tinnitus and air travel](/blog/tinnitus-and-air-travel)** describes.

## <Highlighter>Other reasons your tinnitus may have changed</Highlighter>

Many people on Ozempic are also living with type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure or both, and each of those is part of the tinnitus picture on its own. Our posts on **[tinnitus and diabetes](/blog/tinnitus-and-diabetes-the-blood-sugar-link)** and **[blood pressure and tinnitus](/blog/blood-pressure-and-tinnitus)** cover those links in detail.

Starting a new treatment also tends to come with other changes at once: a new diet, poor sleep during the nausea weeks, more or less caffeine, extra stress about side effects. Any of these can make tinnitus seem louder for a while, which is why it helps to understand **[why tinnitus spikes](/blog/why-does-tinnitus-spike)** before blaming any single cause.

## <Highlighter>What to do if your tinnitus started or got worse on Ozempic</Highlighter>

A calm, step-by-step approach gets you answers faster than either ignoring it or stopping the drug in a panic.

##### Check for warning signs first

- Sudden hearing loss in one ear, especially over hours or a day, needs medical care the same day. Read **[sudden hearing loss and tinnitus](/blog/sudden-hearing-loss-and-tinnitus)** for why timing matters.
- Tinnitus in only one ear that does not go away deserves a check, as **[tinnitus in one ear](/blog/tinnitus-in-one-ear)** explains.
- Severe dizziness, facial weakness or a sound that beats with your pulse should also be seen promptly.

##### Keep a short record

- Note the date the sound started or changed, your dose, and the date of each dose increase.
- Add sleep, nausea or vomiting days, caffeine and noise exposure. A **[tinnitus trigger tracking](/blog/tinnitus-trigger-tracking)** habit makes patterns much easier to spot, and you can log it in the **[Tinnitus Help app](/app)**.

##### Talk to your prescriber

- Do not stop or change the dose on your own. Bring your notes and ask whether the timing fits a dose change.
- Ask for a hearing test if the tinnitus persists. Knowing your baseline is useful, and **[how audiologists diagnose and treat tinnitus](/blog/how-audiologists-diagnose-and-treat-tinnitus)** shows what to expect.
- Report it. The Ozempic label gives the FDA MedWatch line, 1-800-FDA-1088. Voluntary reports are how new side effects get found.

##### Ease the sound in the meantime

- Avoid sitting in total silence, which makes the ringing stand out. A soft background like **[white noise](/zen/white-noise)** at low volume can take the edge off.

## <Highlighter>Conclusion: keep the drug question and the ear question together</Highlighter>

Right now the honest answer is that Ozempic has not been shown to cause tinnitus. The adverse event signal is weak and mostly tied to liraglutide, the drug labels do not mention it, and the only large hearing study found a small increase in hearing loss diagnoses that may not be caused by the drug at all. The one ear effect with a clear mechanism, an open Eustachian tube after fast weight loss, is real, recognisable and treatable.

If you have noticed a change, you do not have to choose between your metabolic health and your ears. Write down what changed and when, keep your prescriber in the loop, and get your hearing checked if the sound sticks around. If worry about the noise is weighing on you, the strategies in **[tinnitus and anxiety](/blog/tinnitus-and-anxiety-staying-mentally-strong)** can help while you get answers.
