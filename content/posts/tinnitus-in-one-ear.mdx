## <Highlighter>Why is my tinnitus only in one ear?</Highlighter>
Most of the time, one-sided tinnitus has an ordinary explanation: earwax against the eardrum, a lingering ear infection, or simply more noise exposure on that side over the years. What makes it different from ringing in both ears is not that it is more dangerous, but that it is more likely to have a specific, findable cause in that one ear. That is why clinical guidelines single it out for a prompt hearing test rather than a wait-and-see approach.

So the honest answer is reassuring with one condition attached. You probably do not have anything serious. You should still get the ear checked, because one-sided tinnitus is the pattern where a hearing test is most likely to find something worth knowing, and where a few causes respond much better to early treatment.

If tinnitus is new to you in general, it helps to start with **[what tinnitus actually is](/blog/what-is-tinnitus)** before narrowing in on the one-sided question. This guide covers what tends to cause tinnitus on a single side, which symptoms change the urgency, what the guidelines recommend, and what to expect when you get it looked at.

## <Highlighter>What causes tinnitus in one ear?</Highlighter>
Your two ears are separate organs with separate histories. They accumulate different amounts of wax, catch infections independently, and are rarely exposed to exactly the same noise. Asymmetry is normal, and it is usually the explanation.

##### Common and generally harmless

- **Earwax against the eardrum.** One of the most frequent causes, and among the easiest to fix. Wax that touches the eardrum can create ringing that disappears entirely once it is removed, which is covered in more depth in our guide to **[earwax and tinnitus](/blog/earwax-and-tinnitus)**.
- **Infection or fluid.** Middle ear infections and fluid behind the eardrum commonly affect one side and often bring muffled hearing with them. See **[ear infections and tinnitus](/blog/can-ear-infections-cause-tinnitus)** for how that usually resolves.
- **Uneven noise exposure.** Drivers often have more hearing loss in the ear nearest the open window, and shooters in the ear facing the muzzle. A single loud event can also do it, which is what happens with **[tinnitus after a concert](/blog/tinnitus-after-a-concert)**.
- **Jaw and neck causes.** Problems in the jaw joint frequently show up on one side only. If your ear noise changes when you clench, chew, or turn your head, read about the **[jaw connection](/blog/tmj-and-tinnitus-the-jaw-connection)**, which covers why movement can alter the sound.

##### Less common, and the reason for checking

- **Sudden sensorineural hearing loss.** Rapid hearing loss that usually affects one ear, often with ringing and a feeling of fullness. This one is time-critical, and the next section explains why.
- **Meniere's disease.** Typically combines one-sided tinnitus with fluctuating hearing, fullness, and episodes of vertigo.
- **Vestibular schwannoma.** A benign, slow-growing tumour on the hearing and balance nerve. It is genuinely rare, and it is the reason guidelines mention imaging for one-sided tinnitus, not a reason to expect it.

One pattern sits in its own category. If the sound beats in time with your heartbeat, that is pulsatile tinnitus, which has a different set of causes related to blood flow and warrants its own evaluation. Our guide to **[pulsatile tinnitus](/blog/pulsatile-tinnitus-why-you-hear-your-heartbeat)** covers what that means.

## <Highlighter>When one-sided tinnitus is urgent</Highlighter>
Most of this can wait for a normal appointment. One scenario cannot.

If one-sided tinnitus arrives together with a sudden drop in hearing on that side, treat it as an emergency and seek care the same day. The NIDCD is explicit that sudden deafness should be considered a medical emergency and that a doctor should be seen immediately. The standard treatment is corticosteroids, and they work best when started as soon as possible. Treatment delayed beyond roughly two to four weeks is much less likely to reverse the hearing loss.

This matters because sudden hearing loss is easy to misread. It frequently affects only one ear, and it often comes with ear fullness, dizziness, or ringing, so it can feel like a blocked ear that will clear on its own. It may not, and the window for effective treatment is narrow. Our guide to **[sudden hearing loss](/blog/sudden-hearing-loss-and-tinnitus)** covers the warning signs in detail.

##### Seek same-day care if you have

- Sudden or rapidly worsening hearing loss in the affected ear
- Tinnitus that beats in time with your pulse
- Facial weakness or numbness on that side
- New or severe balance problems or vertigo

##### Book a routine appointment if

- You have new tinnitus in one ear and none of the above
- The sound has persisted on one side for more than a few weeks
- Your hearing on that side feels different, even subtly

## <Highlighter>What the clinical guidelines actually say</Highlighter>
The American Academy of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery publishes the clinical practice guideline that clinicians follow for tinnitus, and it addresses the one-sided question directly.

On hearing tests, the guideline recommends that clinicians obtain a prompt, comprehensive audiologic examination in patients whose tinnitus is unilateral, persistent for six months or longer, or associated with hearing difficulties. Being one-sided is enough on its own to make the test prompt rather than optional.

On scans, the guideline is deliberately restrictive. It states that clinicians should not obtain imaging of the head and neck specifically to evaluate tinnitus unless the patient has one or more of the following: tinnitus that localises to one ear, pulsatile tinnitus, focal neurological abnormalities, or asymmetric hearing loss.

That phrasing is worth reading carefully, because it cuts both ways. Being one-sided puts you in the group where imaging can be justified. It does not mean everyone with one-sided tinnitus gets an MRI. The guideline notes that routine scans have low yield and real downsides, including cost and incidental findings that lead to further unnecessary testing. The hearing test comes first and usually settles the question.

The guideline also makes a point worth holding onto: tinnitus can be temporary. Loud noise causes a temporary shift in hearing thresholds along with temporary tinnitus, and tinnitus triggered that way will often resolve within a few days.

## <Highlighter>What to expect when you get it checked</Highlighter>
The appointment is more straightforward than people expect, and it is usually reassuring.

Expect a history covering when the sound started, whether your hearing has changed, and any dizziness or fullness, followed by an examination of the ear itself, including removal of any wax blocking the view. Then comes the hearing test, which maps your hearing in each ear separately and shows whether the two sides differ. Asymmetry between ears is the finding that guides whatever comes next.

In most cases the test either finds an ordinary explanation or finds nothing alarming at all. Our guide to **[how audiologists diagnose and treat tinnitus](/blog/how-audiologists-diagnose-and-treat-tinnitus)** walks through the process in more detail, and **[what to do in your first week](/blog/new-tinnitus-what-to-do-first-week)** is useful if the sound is recent.

If the test does show hearing loss on that side, treating it often helps the tinnitus too, because a quieter ear gives the brain less to work with. **[Hearing aids](/blog/hearing-aids-and-tinnitus)** can reduce the perceived volume of tinnitus for exactly that reason.

## <Highlighter>Living with tinnitus on one side</Highlighter>
Once anything urgent has been ruled out, managing one-sided tinnitus is much like managing any tinnitus, with one quirk: because the sound has a location, it can feel easier to fixate on. Your attention keeps returning to that side.

Sound enrichment helps, and it helps for a reason worth understanding. A quiet room makes tinnitus stand out sharply against the silence, while gentle background sound gives your brain something else to attend to. Our guide to **[white noise](/blog/the-power-of-white-noise)** covers how to use it without becoming dependent on it, and a **[notched sound session](/zen/notched-sounds)** is a reasonable place to start if you want to try it tonight.

Sleep deserves specific attention, because a one-sided sound becomes most noticeable when you lie down and one ear is against the pillow. Many people find that positioning changes how prominent it is. **[Tinnitus and sleep](/blog/tinnitus-and-sleep)** covers practical adjustments.

The broader habits matter too. Stress and poor sleep amplify tinnitus regardless of which ear it is in, and the steady routines described in **[managing tinnitus](/blog/managing-tinnitus)** apply equally here. If the sound is driving worry, that is both common and treatable, and **[staying mentally strong](/blog/tinnitus-and-anxiety-staying-mentally-strong)** addresses it directly.

## <Highlighter>Conclusion: get it checked, then get on with it</Highlighter>
One-sided tinnitus sits in an awkward spot. It is usually caused by something ordinary, and simultaneously it is the pattern that clinical guidelines flag for a prompt hearing test. Both things are true, and holding both is the right response: book the appointment, but do not spend the time before it searching for rare diagnoses.

The practical version is short. If your hearing dropped suddenly on that side, seek care today. If the sound beats with your pulse, or you have facial weakness or significant balance problems, seek care today. Otherwise, arrange a hearing test in the coming weeks and treat it as a sensible precaution rather than an emergency.

Most people who do this come away with either a simple fix or straightforward reassurance, and then the task becomes the ordinary one of living well alongside the sound. That is a task with real tools behind it, and a lot of people manage it far better than they expect to at the start.
