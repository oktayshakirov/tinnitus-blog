## <Highlighter>Can a cold cause tinnitus?</Highlighter>
Yes, a cold can cause tinnitus, but it is usually temporary. Swelling in the nose and throat blocks the **Eustachian tube**, so pressure and sometimes fluid build up behind the eardrum. Outside sound gets muffled and your brain notices internal noise more. For most people the ringing fades as the cold clears, typically within one to two weeks.

That is the short version. The longer version matters because a few cold-season ear problems look the same at first but need very different handling. One of them, sudden hearing loss, is a genuine emergency that people regularly mistake for a stuffy ear.

If you are new to ringing in the ears, it helps to know what **[tinnitus](/blog/what-is-tinnitus)** actually is before you worry about it. Below you will learn how a cold reaches your ears, what the ringing tends to sound like, how long it usually lasts, what helps, and the warning signs that mean you should get checked quickly.

## <Highlighter>How a cold reaches your ears</Highlighter>
Your middle ear is not a sealed box. It connects to the back of your nose through the Eustachian tube, a narrow channel that opens when you swallow or yawn to keep the air pressure on both sides of the eardrum equal. A cold inflames exactly the tissue around that opening.

There are four main ways a cold can end up as ringing:

- **A blocked Eustachian tube.** Swelling stops the tube from opening properly. Pressure behind the eardrum drops, the eardrum cannot move freely, and you get fullness, muffled hearing, popping and often a low hum or ringing.
- **Fluid behind the eardrum.** When the tube stays blocked, fluid can collect in the middle ear. The NIDCD notes that blockage from ear infection fluid can trigger tinnitus.
- **A middle ear infection.** According to the NHS, middle ear infections are caused by viruses like colds and flu, and they involve the Eustachian tube. That turns a simple blocked ear into a painful one.
- **Sinusitis after the cold.** The NHS describes sinusitis as common after a cold or flu, and a feeling of pressure in the ears is one of its symptoms. It also lasts longer than a cold.

There is also a brain side to this. When less outside sound reaches the inner ear, the auditory system is thought to turn up its own sensitivity, which makes background signals easier to hear. That is why the ringing often feels loudest in a quiet room at night.

| Cause | What you usually notice | Typical course |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blocked Eustachian tube | Fullness, popping, muffled hearing, low hum | Eases as the congestion clears |
| Fluid behind the eardrum | Muffled hearing, crackling when you swallow | Can linger after the nose clears |
| Middle ear infection | Earache, sometimes fever, reduced hearing | Most clear within 3 days, some up to a week (NHS) |
| Sinusitis | Face pain, thick mucus, ear pressure | Usually clears within 4 weeks (NHS) |

Being ill also brings its own **[tinnitus spike](/blog/why-does-tinnitus-spike)** triggers. Poor sleep, feeling run down and spending all day paying attention to your ears all make existing ringing more noticeable, even when the ears themselves are only mildly affected.

## <Highlighter>What cold-related tinnitus sounds and feels like</Highlighter>
Ringing that comes with a cold tends to arrive with other ear symptoms rather than on its own. That pattern is a useful clue.

##### Common features

- A low hum, buzz or ringing that started after the congestion did
- Ears that feel full, plugged or underwater
- Crackling or popping when you swallow, yawn or blow your nose
- Muffled hearing, so voices sound distant
- Symptoms in both ears, or more on one side

Cold tinnitus can be lopsided, because one Eustachian tube may be more blocked than the other. Still, ringing that stays in **[one ear](/blog/tinnitus-in-one-ear)** after the cold has gone deserves a check rather than more waiting.

It is also worth ruling out lookalikes. Hay fever produces the same Eustachian tube swelling, which is covered in **[tinnitus and allergies](/blog/tinnitus-and-allergies)**. A plug of earwax can cause the same fullness and ringing without any cold at all, and is treated differently, as explained in **[earwax and tinnitus](/blog/earwax-and-tinnitus)**.

## <Highlighter>How long does tinnitus from a cold last?</Highlighter>
The ringing usually follows the cold, with a short delay. The NHS says you should begin to feel better from a cold in about one to two weeks. Ear pressure often takes a few extra days to settle, because the Eustachian tube needs time to open up again and any fluid needs time to drain.

A rough guide:

- **Plain cold with blocked ears:** ringing eases as the congestion clears, often within the same one to two weeks.
- **Cold that turns into a middle ear infection:** the NHS says most ear infections clear up within three days, though symptoms can last up to a week. The ringing tends to settle as the pain and fullness do. There is more detail in **[can ear infections cause tinnitus](/blog/can-ear-infections-cause-tinnitus)**.
- **Cold that turns into sinusitis:** the NHS says sinusitis usually clears within four weeks, so ear pressure and the noise that comes with it can last longer.

If the cold has fully gone but the ringing has not, do not just keep waiting. See your GP. The NIDCD advises that the first step for tinnitus is a primary care visit to check for earwax or fluid from an ear infection blocking the ear.

## <Highlighter>What helps while you recover</Highlighter>
There is no treatment that switches off cold-related tinnitus directly. What helps is easing the congestion so the ear can recover, and making the noise easier to live with until it does.

##### For the congestion

- Rest and drink plenty of fluids, as the NHS recommends for a cold
- Breathe in steam, for example by sitting in the bathroom with a hot shower running
- Rinse your nose with a salt water solution, which the NHS suggests for sinusitis
- Use decongestant nasal sprays for no more than a week, since the NHS warns longer use can make symptoms worse
- Blow your nose gently, one side at a time, rather than forcing air hard against a blocked nose
- Skip antibiotics for a plain cold. The NHS notes they do not relieve symptoms because colds are caused by viruses

##### For the noise

- Avoid total silence. A fan or soft background sound gives your brain something other than the ringing to listen to. **[The power of white noise](/blog/the-power-of-white-noise)** explains why this works, and our **[white noise session](/zen/white-noise)** is an easy place to start.
- Protect your sleep, since being ill and tired makes tinnitus harder to ignore. **[Tinnitus and sleep](/blog/tinnitus-and-sleep)** has practical bedtime routines.
- Remind yourself that this is usually a passing flare, not the start of something permanent. Worry tends to make tinnitus feel louder.

##### About cold medicines

Most cold remedies are fine, but painkillers deserve attention. The NIDCD lists non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen, naproxen and aspirin among medicines associated with tinnitus, especially at high doses. Many cold and flu products already contain a painkiller, and the NHS warns against taking other cold medicines alongside separate paracetamol or ibuprofen tablets, because you can end up taking more than you should. Stay within the label dose, and if the ringing started after a new medicine, mention it to a pharmacist. **[Ototoxic medications and tinnitus](/blog/ototoxic-medications-and-tinnitus)** covers which drugs can affect hearing.

##### If you have to fly

Flying with a blocked Eustachian tube can make ear pressure and ringing much worse during the descent. If a trip falls in the middle of a cold, read **[tinnitus and air travel](/blog/tinnitus-and-air-travel)** before you go.

## <Highlighter>When ringing with a cold needs urgent care</Highlighter>
Most cold-related tinnitus is a nuisance, not a danger. The important exception is **sudden sensorineural hearing loss**, a rapid loss of hearing caused by a problem in the inner ear rather than a blockage.

The NIDCD notes that people with sudden hearing loss often put off seeing a doctor because they assume it is down to allergies, a sinus infection or earwax. Infections are among its known causes, it usually affects one ear, and it often comes with ear fullness, dizziness or ringing. In other words, it can look a lot like a bad cold in one ear.

Timing matters. According to the NIDCD, about half of people recover some or all of their hearing on their own, but treatment delayed by more than two to four weeks is less likely to reverse the loss. That is why it should be treated as a medical emergency. You can read more in **[sudden hearing loss and tinnitus](/blog/sudden-hearing-loss-and-tinnitus)**.

##### Get help the same day if you notice

- A sudden or clear drop in hearing, especially in one ear
- Dizziness or a spinning feeling along with the ringing
- Swelling around the ear, or fluid coming out of it
- A very high temperature, or feeling very unwell
- Earache with a weakened immune system or a long-term condition such as diabetes

The NHS lists all of these, including any change in hearing or new hearing loss, among the reasons to get an urgent GP appointment or call 111 when you have ear infection symptoms.

## <Highlighter>If the ringing outlasts the cold</Highlighter>
Sometimes the congestion is long gone and the ringing is still there. That does not automatically mean lasting damage, but it does mean it is time to stop waiting it out.

Your GP can look in your ears for leftover fluid, infection or wax, and refer you for a hearing test if needed. An audiologist can then measure your hearing and match the tinnitus to its likely cause. **[How audiologists diagnose and treat tinnitus](/blog/how-audiologists-diagnose-and-treat-tinnitus)** walks through what that appointment involves.

In the meantime, treat it the way you would any new tinnitus: keep some sound around you, protect your sleep and try not to monitor it all day. The calm, practical steps in **[managing tinnitus](/blog/managing-tinnitus)** apply here too, and they work whether the ringing ends up lasting days or longer.

## <Highlighter>Conclusion: a cold usually rings, then quiets</Highlighter>
A cold can absolutely cause tinnitus. The swelling that blocks your nose also blocks the small tube that keeps your middle ear ventilated, and the pressure, fluid and muffled hearing that follow make ringing easier to hear. For most people it is temporary and fades as the cold clears.

While you recover, focus on the congestion, keep gentle sound in the background and watch your painkiller doses. Take one symptom seriously above all others: a sudden drop in hearing is not a blocked ear until a doctor says so. If the ringing stays after the cold has gone, book a check rather than waiting, and lean on steady habits like sound enrichment and good sleep while you find out more.
