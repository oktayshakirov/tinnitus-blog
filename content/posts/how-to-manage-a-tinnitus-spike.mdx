## <Highlighter>How do you manage a tinnitus spike?</Highlighter>
To manage a tinnitus spike, calm the alarm rather than fight the sound: put on a soft background sound instead of sitting in silence, slow your breathing, release your jaw and shoulders, and give your attention something absorbing to do. Then protect tonight's sleep, note what came before the spike, and know the few warning signs that need a doctor.

That is the whole plan in two sentences. The rest of this guide explains each step, because a step you understand is one you will actually use when the ringing is at its loudest and your thinking is at its least clear.

If you want to understand the triggers and brain mechanisms in depth, that is covered in **[why does tinnitus spike](/blog/why-does-tinnitus-spike)**. This guide is the practical companion: what to do, in what order, and what to avoid.

## <Highlighter>What a spike is, and what it is not</Highlighter>
A **tinnitus spike** is a temporary rise in how loud, sharp or intrusive your tinnitus feels. It might follow a noisy evening, a short night, a stressful week, or a cold. Sometimes the trigger comes a day before the spike, which is why so many feel as if they came from nowhere.

The most important thing to know is that a spike on its own is not proof of new damage. Tinnitus naturally rises and falls, and the way your brain processes sound plays a large part in how loud it seems at any moment. When your nervous system is on alert, it pays closer attention to every signal it can find, including the one coming from inside your own auditory system. You can read more about that process in **[what role does the brain play in tinnitus](/blog/what-role-does-the-brain-play-in-tinnitus)**.

That is why the steps below do not try to switch the sound off. They work on the alert underneath it. The NHS notes that focusing on tinnitus can make it worse, and the American Tinnitus Association says that activities which lower stress may also lessen the intensity of tinnitus symptoms. A spike is the moment both of those facts matter most.

## <Highlighter>The first ten minutes</Highlighter>
Before anything else, take ten seconds to rule out the urgent signs listed further down: sudden hearing loss, vertigo, facial weakness, a recent head injury, or a sound that pulses with your heartbeat. If none of those apply, work through these five steps in order.

##### 1. Name it

Say to yourself, plainly, "this is a spike, not a setback." It sounds trivial, but fear is one more signal telling your brain to stay on alert. Labelling the experience gives you a little distance from it, and that distance is where the other steps start to work.

##### 2. Breathe slowly

The NHS breathing exercise for stress is a good template. Sit or lie somewhere supported, let the breath reach down into your belly without forcing it, breathe in gently through your nose for a steady count of five, and out through your mouth for a count of five. Keep going for at least five minutes. If a slightly longer breath out feels more comfortable, that is fine too. The NHS tinnitus guidance itself lists deep breathing and yoga as things that may help.

##### 3. Add a gentle sound

Silence is the worst setting for a spike, because it leaves nothing for your brain to listen to except the tinnitus. NHS guidance is direct about it: do not have total silence. Soft music, rain, a fan, or a low **[white noise](/blog/the-power-of-white-noise)** all work. The next section covers how loud it should be.

##### 4. Unclench

Drop your shoulders, let your tongue rest away from the roof of your mouth, and let your teeth part slightly. According to the US National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders (NIDCD), clenching your jaw or grinding your teeth can cause or worsen tinnitus, and the link is explored further in **[TMJ and tinnitus](/blog/tmj-and-tinnitus-the-jaw-connection)**. Many people only discover how tense they are when a spike makes them check.

##### 5. Give your attention a job

Checking the sound every thirty seconds keeps it in the spotlight. Choose something that genuinely absorbs you: a walk, a call with a friend, cooking, a podcast, a puzzle. The NHS suggests hobbies and activities as a way to take your mind off tinnitus. The goal is not to prove the sound has gone, only to stop measuring it.

## <Highlighter>How loud should the background sound be?</Highlighter>
This is the part most people get wrong in one direction or the other. Too quiet and it does nothing. Too loud and you are simply swapping one noise you dislike for another, often at a volume that is uncomfortable in its own right.

A practical rule: set the sound low and comfortable, so it blends with your tinnitus rather than covering it completely, and so you can still hear the room around you. NIDCD describes sound therapy as working in a few ways, by masking tinnitus, distracting you from it, or helping you get used to it. During a spike you are mostly after the second and third, and a sound that blends does that without drowning out the world.

| Setting | What it does | Try instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total silence | Leaves the tinnitus as the only thing to hear | Any soft, steady sound in the room |
| Loud masking through headphones | Hides the sound, but adds more noise and makes taking them off a shock | A speaker or fan at a low level |
| Low, blended sound | Takes the edge off while keeping you in touch with your surroundings | This is the target |

The type of sound matters less than whether you find it pleasant. Some people prefer water, others a deeper hum. Our **[water sounds](/zen/water-sounds)** session is a simple place to start, and if you are curious why silence feels so much worse, **[can silence make tinnitus worse](/blog/can-silence-make-tinnitus-worse)** goes into it.

## <Highlighter>The rest of the day and tonight</Highlighter>
Once the first wave has passed, the aim shifts from calming the moment to not adding fuel.

- **Give your ears a quieter day.** If the spike followed a gig or a loud event, steer clear of more loud noise for now. The NIDCD advises limiting loud noise exposure to avoid making symptoms worse, and **[tinnitus after a concert](/blog/tinnitus-after-a-concert)** covers that specific situation.
- **Go easy on caffeine and alcohol if you suspect them.** The American Tinnitus Association notes there is very little scientific evidence that caffeine worsens tinnitus, and recommends tracking your own response instead. If you are already on edge, an extra coffee is unlikely to help your sleep, which matters more.
- **Protect your sleep.** NHS guidance suggests a regular bedtime routine and cutting down on caffeine. Keep the same bedtime, dim the lights, and keep a soft sound running in the bedroom rather than lying in silence. **[Tinnitus and sleep](/blog/tinnitus-and-sleep)** has a full routine if nights are where spikes hit hardest.
- **Move.** The ATA notes that exercise reduces stress, which can exacerbate tinnitus. A brisk walk in the evening does double duty: it calms you and tires you out.

## <Highlighter>After it settles, find the pattern</Highlighter>
The best time to learn from a spike is while it is still fresh. Take two minutes and write down how you slept over the last two nights, your stress level out of ten, any loud noise, caffeine and alcohol, and anything unusual such as a cold or a new medication.

One note will not tell you much, because tinnitus varies for no obvious reason. A few weeks of short notes will. A spike on a Tuesday sometimes turns out to have started with a short night on the Sunday, and a spike you can trace is far less frightening than one that seems random. **[Tinnitus trigger tracking](/blog/tinnitus-trigger-tracking)** explains what to log and how to read it, and the **[Tinnitus Help app](/app)** can keep the record for you.

Do not cut out foods or habits just because they came before one bad day. The ATA's advice on diet is to track your own experience and adjust, which means looking for repeated patterns, not single coincidences.

## <Highlighter>Common mistakes that keep a spike going</Highlighter>
Most of these are natural reactions, which is exactly why they are worth naming.

- **Hunting for silence.** It feels like the cure and works like the opposite.
- **Checking constantly.** Every check is a small instruction to your brain that this sound is important.
- **Searching for worst-case stories at 2 am.** Forums are full of people whose tinnitus got worse, because people whose spike settled rarely post about it.
- **Masking at high volume.** Loud sound through headphones may feel like relief, but it adds to your noise exposure.
- **Making big changes in a panic.** Stopping a prescribed medication on your own because of a spike can cause its own problems. Talk to your doctor first.

## <Highlighter>When a spike is more than a spike</Highlighter>
Most spikes are temporary. A few signs mean you should not wait it out, and these come from NHS guidance.

##### Go to A and E or call 999 if your tinnitus

- follows a head injury
- comes with sudden hearing loss in one or both ears
- comes with weakness in the muscles of your face
- comes with a spinning sensation (vertigo)

Sudden hearing loss is time sensitive, and **[sudden hearing loss and tinnitus](/blog/sudden-hearing-loss-and-tinnitus)** explains why fast treatment matters.

##### Ask for an urgent GP appointment if

- the sound beats in time with your pulse, which is covered in **[pulsatile tinnitus](/blog/pulsatile-tinnitus-why-you-hear-your-heartbeat)**

##### Book a routine GP appointment if

- you have tinnitus regularly or constantly
- it is getting worse over time
- it is affecting your sleep, concentration or mood

## <Highlighter>When spikes keep coming back</Highlighter>
If spikes are frequent, the most useful long-term step is not a better spike routine but a change in how much power the sound has over you. The treatment with the strongest evidence for that is **cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)**.

A 2020 Cochrane review of 28 studies with 2,733 adults found that CBT may reduce the impact tinnitus has on quality of life compared with no treatment, though the certainty of that evidence is low, and there is not yet data on how long the benefit lasts. The review did not report effects on loudness, so CBT is best understood as making the sound matter less rather than making it quieter. In practice, that is often what decides whether a spike ruins a week or a few hours. **[Cognitive behavioral therapy and tinnitus](/blog/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-and-tinnitus)** explains what a course involves, and the NHS notes that digital CBT is often offered first.

If worry about the next spike has become a problem in its own right, the ideas in **[tinnitus and anxiety: staying mentally strong](/blog/tinnitus-and-anxiety-staying-mentally-strong)** are a good place to begin while you wait for an appointment.

## <Highlighter>Conclusion: a spike is a flare-up, not a verdict</Highlighter>
The next time your tinnitus spikes, try doing the opposite of what instinct says. Not a silent room and a close listen, but a soft sound, a slow breath, loose shoulders and something else to think about. Then look after tonight's sleep, jot down what came before, and keep the warning signs in mind.

Spikes feel endless while they are happening. Writing them down is the best way to see, over weeks, that each one passed and that most had a cause you can work with. If they keep coming back or the worry is wearing you down, talk to your GP or an audiologist about CBT and sound therapy. You do not have to handle this alone.
