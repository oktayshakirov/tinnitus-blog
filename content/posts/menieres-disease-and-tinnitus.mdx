## <Highlighter>Does Meniere's Disease Cause Tinnitus?</Highlighter>
Yes. Tinnitus is one of the four defining symptoms of **Meniere's disease**, an inner ear disorder that also causes spinning vertigo, fluctuating hearing loss and a feeling of fullness in the ear. It usually affects one ear, often sounds like a low roar, and tends to flare around attacks before becoming more constant over the years.

If you are reading this, you may have just had your first frightening attack, or you may have lived with a one-sided roar for a while and only now heard the name. Either way, the uncertainty is often worse than the facts. Meniere's is a long-term condition, but it is well understood, it follows a fairly predictable course, and there is a lot you can do about both the attacks and the noise.

This guide covers what is happening inside the ear, how Meniere's tinnitus differs from more common types of **[tinnitus](/blog/what-is-tinnitus)**, how doctors confirm the diagnosis, and which treatments have evidence behind them.

## <Highlighter>What Happens in the Ear</Highlighter>
Deep inside your inner ear sits the **labyrinth**, a set of fluid-filled tubes that handle both hearing and balance. In Meniere's disease, symptoms are associated with a buildup of one of those fluids, called endolymph. This swelling is known as **endolymphatic hydrops**, and it disrupts the balance and hearing signals the ear sends to your brain.

Why the fluid builds up is still unclear. According to the US National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders (NIDCD), proposed explanations include constricted blood vessels (a mechanism also linked to migraine), viral infections, allergies and autoimmune reactions. Because it sometimes runs in families, genetic factors may play a part too.

When the hearing side of the labyrinth is disturbed, the brain receives a weaker and distorted signal from that ear. As with other forms of hearing-related tinnitus, the auditory system appears to respond by generating its own sound. That is why the tinnitus in Meniere's so often travels together with the hearing changes, and why it is usually on the same side.

Some numbers help put it in context:

| Fact | What the sources say |
| --- | --- |
| How many people | About 615,000 people in the US, with around 45,500 new diagnoses a year (NIDCD) |
| Typical age | Most likely to start between 40 and 60; rare in children |
| One ear or both | Usually one ear; both ears in 15% to 25% of people (NIDCD) |
| Attack length | From a few minutes up to 24 hours (NHS) |

## <Highlighter>What Meniere's Tinnitus Sounds Like</Highlighter>
Meniere's tinnitus is classically described as a **low-pitched, roaring** sound, more like a seashell held to the ear or the hum of distant machinery than the thin, high whistle most people imagine. The StatPearls clinical reference on Meniere's describes the low roar as typical, and contrasts it with a high-pitched ringing that may point to a different cause.

It is not identical for everyone. Some people hear a tone, some a hiss, and some a mix. The pattern over time is more telling than the exact pitch:

- **Early on**, the tinnitus often comes and goes, swelling during an attack together with the fullness and hearing loss, then easing afterwards.
- **Over the years**, NHS guidance notes that hearing in the affected ear may gradually worsen and constant tinnitus may develop.
- **On one side.** Because Meniere's usually affects a single ear, so does the tinnitus. If one-sided ringing is your main concern, our guide to **[tinnitus in one ear](/blog/tinnitus-in-one-ear)** explains the other causes doctors consider.

That mix of a loud roar, pressure and muffled hearing during an attack is a big part of why Meniere's feels so disorienting. It is also why tracking your episodes is useful: writing down when the roar rises, how long the vertigo lasts and what your hearing was like gives your specialist far more to work with. A simple **[tinnitus trigger tracking](/blog/tinnitus-trigger-tracking)** log works well for this.

## <Highlighter>How Meniere's Is Diagnosed</Highlighter>
There is no single test for Meniere's disease. An ENT specialist (otolaryngologist) puts the diagnosis together from your history and a hearing test, while ruling out conditions that look similar. NIDCD lists the criteria for a **definite** diagnosis as:

- Two or more spontaneous vertigo attacks, each lasting **20 minutes to 12 hours**
- Low to medium frequency hearing loss confirmed on a hearing test
- Fluctuating ear symptoms in the affected ear, such as tinnitus or fullness
- No other balance disorder that better explains it

When everything fits except the hearing test, doctors call it **probable** Meniere's. A full workup usually includes the hearing assessment you would expect from **[how audiologists diagnose and treat tinnitus](/blog/how-audiologists-diagnose-and-treat-tinnitus)**, and often an MRI scan. StatPearls notes that an MRI with contrast is required when there is one-sided sensorineural hearing loss, to exclude a benign nerve tumour called an acoustic neuroma.

##### Conditions that can look like Meniere's

- **Vestibular migraine**, which StatPearls calls the most common mimic. If headaches, light sensitivity or visual aura come with your dizzy spells, read up on **[migraine and tinnitus](/blog/migraine-and-tinnitus)** and mention it to your doctor.
- **Labyrinthitis, vestibular neuritis and ear infections**, which the NHS lists among the conditions with similar symptoms.
- **Sudden sensorineural hearing loss**, a rapid drop in hearing that needs urgent treatment. Our article on **[sudden hearing loss and tinnitus](/blog/sudden-hearing-loss-and-tinnitus)** explains why the first few days matter.

##### When to get urgent help

The NHS advises calling emergency services if spinning comes with a drooping face, weakness in an arm, slurred speech, sudden vision loss in one eye or feeling very off-balance, because these can be signs of a stroke. Do not drive yourself. A sudden drop in hearing in one ear also deserves same-day medical attention.

## <Highlighter>Treatment: What Helps the Attacks</Highlighter>
Meniere's cannot currently be cured, but treatment aims to reduce how often attacks happen and how severe they are. Doctors generally work up a ladder, starting with the least invasive options.

| Step | What it involves | What to know |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lifestyle | Limiting salt to about 1,500 to 2,000 mg a day; cutting caffeine, alcohol and smoking | Standard advice, but StatPearls notes no trials show salt restriction prevents attacks |
| Tablets | Diuretics, attack medicines for vertigo and nausea; betahistine in some countries | A 2023 Cochrane review of 10 studies (848 people) found it unclear whether oral medicines help |
| Injections into the middle ear | Corticosteroids, or the antibiotic gentamicin for stubborn vertigo | Steroids carry little hearing risk; gentamicin controls vertigo better but raises the risk of hearing loss |
| Surgery | Endolymphatic sac decompression, or cutting the balance nerve | Reserved for attacks that other treatments have not controlled |
| Rehabilitation | Vestibular rehabilitation exercises | Recommended for lingering balance problems between attacks |

A few points are worth stressing. Betahistine is widely prescribed in the UK and Europe but is not approved by the FDA in the United States, and the Cochrane reviewers rated the evidence for it as low or very low certainty. Gentamicin works by deliberately damaging the balance cells, which is why it carries a hearing risk; it belongs to the same family of drugs covered in our guide to **[ototoxic medications and tinnitus](/blog/ototoxic-medications-and-tinnitus)**, and should only be used under specialist care.

The lifestyle advice overlaps with what many people already try for tinnitus. If you suspect coffee or alcohol makes your ear worse, our articles on **[caffeine and tinnitus](/blog/caffeine-and-tinnitus)** and **[the impact of smoking and alcohol](/blog/the-impact-of-smoking-and-alcohol)** go into the details. Make changes with your doctor rather than slashing everything at once.

NIDCD is also clear on what is not supported: there is no evidence that acupuncture, acupressure, tai chi or herbal supplements such as ginkgo biloba, niacin or ginger root help Meniere's disease.

## <Highlighter>Managing the Tinnitus Itself</Highlighter>
Here is the honest part: most Meniere's treatments target the vertigo, not the tinnitus. The Cochrane review focused on vertigo because that is what the trials measured. So while you work on attack control with your specialist, it pays to treat the tinnitus as its own problem, using the same tools that help tinnitus from any cause.

##### Hearing support

Hearing loss and tinnitus feed each other. Both NIDCD and the NHS list hearing aids as an option for Meniere's, and amplifying real-world sound gives the brain something other than the roar to listen to. Our guide to **[hearing aids and tinnitus](/blog/hearing-aids-and-tinnitus)** covers what to ask an audiologist, including combination devices with built-in sound generators.

##### Sound therapy

A quiet room makes a low roar stand out. Gentle background sound at a level just below your tinnitus reduces that contrast, which is especially helpful in the evening. You can try our **[White Noise](/zen/white-noise)** session or a soft fan, and adjust the volume and texture until the roar sits comfortably underneath.

##### Calming the stress loop

Unpredictable attacks are frightening, and fear of the next one keeps the nervous system on high alert, which makes tinnitus harder to ignore. The NHS recommends counselling, relaxation techniques and breathing exercises for the emotional side of Meniere's. **[Cognitive behavioral therapy and tinnitus](/blog/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-and-tinnitus)** has the strongest evidence of any approach for reducing how much tinnitus bothers you, and our guide to **[tinnitus and anxiety](/blog/tinnitus-and-anxiety-staying-mentally-strong)** offers practical tools you can start today.

<Image src="/images/sound-therapy-headphones.jpg" alt="Woman lying back on a sofa with white headphones on and her hand on her head, resting with calming background sound to ease a tinnitus flare." />

##### Living with attacks

The NHS offers practical self-care advice: keep your medicines with you, think about the risks before swimming, climbing ladders or using dangerous machinery, and do not drive if you feel dizzy or an attack is starting. In the UK, you must tell the DVLA if you have sudden or frequent dizziness.

## <Highlighter>The Long-Term Outlook</Highlighter>
Meniere's disease changes over time, and not only for the worse. According to StatPearls, vertigo attacks generally become less frequent and shorter as the years go by, and tend to settle on their own after a median of around eight years. Hearing and balance decline mostly within the first five to ten years.

The trade-off is that hearing loss in the affected ear usually becomes permanent, and constant tinnitus often comes with it. Both ears become involved in a minority of people. That is exactly why investing early in hearing support and tinnitus coping skills pays off: they keep working long after the vertigo has quietened down.

## <Highlighter>Conclusion: Treat the Attacks, Train the Ear</Highlighter>
Meniere's disease brings tinnitus along with vertigo, fullness and hearing loss, usually in one ear and often as a low roar that rises with each attack. The first priority is a proper diagnosis from an ENT specialist, so that look-alikes such as vestibular migraine, sudden hearing loss or an acoustic neuroma are ruled out.

From there, think of it as two jobs. Your specialist works on reducing the attacks, starting with lifestyle steps and moving up the ladder only if needed. Meanwhile, you can work on the tinnitus directly with hearing support, sound enrichment and stress skills. Our guide to **[managing tinnitus](/blog/managing-tinnitus)** is a good next step, and the **[Tinnitus Help app](/app)** can help you log attacks and spot patterns over time. Many people with Meniere's go on to live full, active lives, and the roar tends to matter less once you have a plan.
