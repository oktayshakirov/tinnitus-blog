## <Highlighter>Can a concussion cause tinnitus?</Highlighter>

Yes. Tinnitus is one of the more common ear symptoms after a concussion. The blow can disturb the inner ear, the hearing nerve or the parts of the brain that process sound, and a neck injury from the same impact can add to it. For many people it fades as the concussion heals, but not for everyone.

If you are reading this a few days after a fall, a car accident or a hit on the sports field, the sound can feel like one more thing going wrong at a time when you already feel foggy and tired. It is worth knowing that you are not imagining it, and that doctors recognise the link. If you are new to the condition itself, **[what is tinnitus](/blog/what-is-tinnitus)** explains the basics.

This guide covers how often tinnitus follows a head injury, the ways an impact can affect your hearing, the warning signs that need urgent care, what a proper check looks like and how to manage the sound while you recover.

## <Highlighter>How common is tinnitus after a concussion?</Highlighter>

Studies agree that it is common, but they disagree on exactly how common, because they use different groups of people and different ways of asking about symptoms. A review of trauma-related tinnitus by researchers at the University of Regensburg reported that up to 53% of people with a traumatic brain injury develop tinnitus.

One of the clearest comparisons comes from Massachusetts Eye and Ear in Boston. Researchers surveyed 52 adults who had a mild traumatic brain injury and 55 adults who had not. They deliberately left out anyone with blast injuries, ear disease or noisy jobs, so that noise exposure could not explain the result. On average the survey was taken almost six years after the injury.

| Symptom reported | After mild brain injury | No brain injury |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tinnitus | 61.5% | 16.4% |
| Hearing problems | 61.5% | 12.7% |
| Sensitivity to everyday sounds | 67.3% | 8.3% |

Two things stand out. First, tinnitus was almost four times as common in the injured group, years after the event. Second, sound sensitivity was even more common than ringing, which is why **[hyperacusis and tinnitus](/blog/hyperacusis-and-tinnitus)** often show up together after a head injury.

A 2026 study from the Football Players Health Study at Harvard adds a dose effect. Among 1,085 former professional American football players, those who reported the most concussion symptoms during their careers had about 2.9 times the odds of tinnitus compared with those who reported the fewest. Repeated knocks seem to matter, not just a single big one.

## <Highlighter>How a head injury makes your ears ring</Highlighter>

A concussion is often described as a brain injury, but the hearing system sits right next to the impact and has several weak points. A 2024 review in the Journal of Neurotrauma describes the main routes.

##### Inner ear concussion

The force of the blow can shake the delicate inner ear even when a scan shows no fracture. Doctors call this **labyrinthine** or **cochlear concussion**. It can cause hearing loss, dizziness and tinnitus, and in animal studies it damaged the sensory hair cells that turn sound into nerve signals.

##### A leak of inner ear fluid

Head trauma can tear one of the thin membranes that seal the inner ear, letting fluid escape. This is called a **perilymphatic fistula**. According to the same review, symptoms typically begin between 24 and 70 hours after the injury, which is one reason tinnitus can appear days later rather than straight away.

##### Fractures and middle ear damage

A harder impact can crack the temporal bone that houses the ear, or disrupt the tiny bones and eardrum of the middle ear. Some of these injuries, such as a torn eardrum or a broken chain of ear bones, can be repaired surgically, so they are worth finding.

##### Delayed fluid problems

The review also describes a post-traumatic form of the fluid build-up seen in **[Ménière's disease and tinnitus](/blog/menieres-disease-and-tinnitus)**, with attacks of vertigo, fullness and ringing that can begin long after the injury itself.

##### Changes in the brain

Even when the ear is intact, the injury can affect how the brain processes sound. Some people with concussion have normal hearing tests but struggle to follow speech in noise, and some show slowed responses in specialised tests of the hearing pathway.

##### The neck and jaw

Whiplash often comes with a concussion, and the Regensburg review found that neck injuries can cause tinnitus on their own. The nerves from the upper neck feed into the same brainstem area that handles hearing, which is the basis of **[somatic tinnitus](/blog/somatic-tinnitus)**. If your tinnitus changes when you turn your head or clench your jaw, this route may be part of the picture.

##### Stress and trauma

The same review points out that emotional trauma and post-traumatic stress can contribute. An accident is frightening, and a nervous system on high alert is more likely to notice and fixate on a new internal sound.

## <Highlighter>Warning signs that need urgent care</Highlighter>

Ringing in the ears on its own is not usually an emergency. The problem is that it can arrive alongside signs that are, so it helps to know the difference.

##### Call 999 or go to A and E

The NHS lists these signs after a head injury as reasons to get emergency help:

- Problems with hearing or vision
- Clear fluid coming from the ears or nose, or bleeding from the ears
- Bruising behind the ears, or a black eye with no direct injury to the eye
- Being knocked out and not waking up, struggling to stay awake, or having a seizure
- New numbness or weakness, or trouble with balance, walking, speaking or understanding

The NHS also advises calling 999 after a fall from more than one metre or five stairs, or a high speed impact such as a road accident.

##### A sound that beats with your pulse

A whooshing or thumping in time with your heartbeat after a head or neck injury needs prompt attention. The Regensburg review warns that this kind of tinnitus can be a sign of serious blood vessel injuries, such as a tear in a neck artery. **[Pulsatile tinnitus](/blog/pulsatile-tinnitus-why-you-hear-your-heartbeat)** explains how it differs from ordinary ringing.

##### Sudden hearing loss or one-sided ringing

If one ear suddenly sounds muffled, do not wait to see whether it passes. Hearing loss can respond to early treatment, which is why **[sudden hearing loss and tinnitus](/blog/sudden-hearing-loss-and-tinnitus)** stresses acting the same day. Ringing that stays in one ear only also deserves a check, as **[tinnitus in one ear](/blog/tinnitus-in-one-ear)** describes.

## <Highlighter>Will tinnitus after a concussion go away?</Highlighter>

The honest answer is that nobody can promise a timeline. The NHS says concussion symptoms can last a few weeks, and it advises seeing a GP if symptoms from a minor head injury last longer than two weeks. For many people the ringing quietens as the headache, fatigue and brain fog lift.

For others it lingers. In the Boston study, people were still reporting tinnitus nearly six years after their injury on average. The 2024 review also notes that tinnitus can appear late and can get worse with repeated concussions. That is a strong reason to protect your head while you recover and to return to contact sport only when a doctor clears you.

Persistent does not mean unchanging. Even when the sound stays, how loud it seems and how much it bothers you can ease with time and the right support, which is what the next two sections are about.

## <Highlighter>Getting checked: what a proper assessment looks like</Highlighter>

Many people with concussion never have their ears looked at. The 2024 review cites research showing that more than half of patients with a mild brain injury do not see a provider within three months. If your tinnitus has lasted beyond the first couple of weeks, or came with any hearing change or dizziness, ask your doctor for a referral to an audiologist or an ENT specialist.

A typical assessment includes:

- A full history: how the injury happened, when the sound started, whether it is in one ear or both, and whether moving your neck or jaw changes it
- An ear examination to look for blood behind the eardrum or a perforation
- A hearing test, which **[how audiologists diagnose and treat tinnitus](/blog/how-audiologists-diagnose-and-treat-tinnitus)** walks through step by step
- Balance testing if you have dizziness

Be aware that a standard hearing test is often normal after a mild brain injury, even when you clearly notice a problem. If your results come back normal but you still struggle, the review describes more specialised tests of the hearing pathway, and a CT or MRI scan may be used if a fracture or fluid leak is suspected. A normal audiogram does not mean your symptoms are not real.

## <Highlighter>Managing tinnitus while you recover from a concussion</Highlighter>

There is no treatment made specifically for post-concussion tinnitus, so management follows the same well-tested principles used for tinnitus in general, adapted to a brain that is still healing.

##### Keep your environment gently sound-filled

Total silence makes ringing stand out, but loud noise can be tiring after a concussion. A soft, steady background such as **[white noise](/zen/white-noise)** at a low volume can take the edge off without overwhelming you.

##### Protect your sleep

Concussion often disrupts sleep, and poor sleep makes tinnitus harder to ignore. A wind-down routine, a dark room and quiet sound at night all help, and **[tinnitus and sleep](/blog/tinnitus-and-sleep)** has more practical ideas.

##### Treat the neck if it is involved

If turning your head or pressing on your neck changes the sound, a physiotherapist who works with whiplash may help. Posture and muscle tension also play a role, as **[can neck tension cause tinnitus](/blog/can-neck-tension-cause-tinnitus)** explains.

##### Look after your mood

The Harvard football study found that links between concussion history and anxiety or depression were stronger in players who also had tinnitus. Worry and low mood are part of recovery for many people, and they are treatable. **[Cognitive behavioral therapy and tinnitus](/blog/cognitive-behavioral-therapy-and-tinnitus)** covers the approach with the strongest evidence for reducing tinnitus distress, and **[tinnitus and anxiety](/blog/tinnitus-and-anxiety-staying-mentally-strong)** offers day-to-day coping strategies.

##### Track how it changes

A simple note of the sound each day, alongside sleep, screen time, stress and noise, helps you and your doctor see whether things are improving. You can log it in the **[Tinnitus Help app](/app)**.

##### Avoid another head injury

Because tinnitus can get worse after repeated concussions, follow your doctor's guidance on returning to sport, cycling or other activities with a risk of falls.

## <Highlighter>Conclusion: take the ringing seriously, then give it time</Highlighter>

A concussion can cause tinnitus through the inner ear, the hearing pathways in the brain, the neck and the stress of the injury itself. It is common, it is recognised, and for many people it eases as the rest of the concussion heals. What matters most in the early days is spotting the signs that need urgent care: hearing loss, fluid from the ears, a pulsing sound or any worsening neurological symptoms.

If the ringing is still there after a couple of weeks, ask for a proper hearing assessment rather than waiting it out alone. Even when the sound lingers, its hold on you can shrink with good sleep, gentle sound, support for your mood and time. The approach in **[tinnitus retraining therapy](/blog/tinnitus-retraining-therapy)** is one well-established way to help your brain learn to tune it out.
